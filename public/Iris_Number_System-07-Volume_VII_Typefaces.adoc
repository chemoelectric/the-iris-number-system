== Prelude: Foundations of Digital Typeface Design in the Iris Framework

Digital typeface design and font outline geometry operate inherently within discrete coordinate grids \( \mathcal{G}_N \) and multiscale rasterization apertures. This volume establishes the rigorous mathematical and algorithmic principles of digital letterform design, curve formulation, and discrete metric rasterization strictly within the Counting-Iris framework and the planar geometric algebra \( Cl(2,0) \).

== Smooth Spline Interpolation: Hobby’s Algorithm and its Extension via Singular Value Decomposition

=== The Typeface Designer’s Challenge: Drawing Curves That Flow

Every digital typeface designer knows the intimate friction of shaping a curve. When drawing the outer swell of a capital *O*, the fluid spine of an *S*, or the delicate teardrop terminal of a lowercase *a*, the designer works with two distinct kinds of points:

1. **On-curve knots** (or master nodes): The anchors through which the curve must pass, typically placed at optical extrema (top, bottom, left, and right baselines and apexes) and inflection points on the discrete design grid \( \mathcal{G}_N \).
2. **Off-curve control points** (often called handles or direction points): The levers that govern the slope and speed of the curve as it departs from and arrives at each anchor.

In standard digital font formats, every smooth outline is encoded as a sequence of polynomial segments—predominantly cubic Bézier curves. 

Adjusting hundreds of individual control handles manually across multiple masters (light, regular, bold, condensed) is exhausting and error-prone. A designer instinctively wants to place the key anchor knots on the discrete design grid and let the software compute the most natural, harmonious curve through them. 

However, standard mathematical curve-fitting techniques often fail the typographic eye:

* **Simple cubic splines** tend to “overshoot” aggressively, creating bloated loops when points are spaced unevenly.
* **Pure circular arcs** lack optical tension, appearing stiff and mechanical.
* **Clothoids and Euler spirals** are mathematically elegant, but classical implementations are computationally heavy and difficult to convert directly into standard cubic Bézier control points without loss of fidelity.

In the mid-1980s, computer scientist John D. Hobby (working closely with Donald Knuth on the Metafont and MetaPost typographic systems) invented a spline-fitting algorithm that captures the intuition of a traditional draftsperson’s flexible physical spline—a thin, elastic strip of wood or steel pinned at specific points. Hobby’s algorithm calculates ideal Bézier handle placements that maintain optical curvature continuity while avoiding unnatural bulges.

=== Anatomy of a Bézier Segment: How Handles Govern Curvature

To understand how Hobby’s method works, we consider a single segment running between two consecutive on-curve knots, which we denote as \( z_0 \) and \( z_1 \).

In the planar Clifford algebra \( Cl(2,0) \), each point is a 2D position multivector on the discrete resolution grid \( \mathcal{G}_N \):
\[ z = x \, e_1 + y \, e_2 \]
A cubic Bézier curve running from \( z_0 \) to \( z_1 \) is governed by two intermediate off-curve control points, \( u_0 \) (the first handle, pulling from \( z_0 \)) and \( v_1 \) (the second handle, pulling toward \( z_1 \)). 

The curve is parameterized by a progress variable \( t \in [0, 1] \) on the discrete evaluation grid:
\[ z(t) = (1 - t)^3 z_0 + 3 (1 - t)^2 t \, u_0 + 3 (1 - t) t^2 \, v_1 + t^3 z_1 \]

==== The Straight Chord and Turning Angles

Let \( \Delta z_0 = z_1 - z_0 \) be the straight chord vector connecting the two knots. 

* The chord length is \( d_0 = | \Delta z_0 | = \sqrt{ (x_1 - x_0)^2 + (y_1 - y_0)^2 } \).
* The chord angle relative to the horizontal basis vector \( e_1 \) is \( \phi_0 = \operatorname{atan2}(y_1 - y_0, \, x_1 - x_0) \).

The direction of the curve as it leaves \( z_0 \) makes an angle \( \theta_0 \) with the chord. Similarly, as the curve enters \( z_1 \), its direction makes an angle \( \psi_1 \) with the chord.

The turning angle of the polygon formed by the knots at node \( k \) is the difference between consecutive chord directions:
\[ \psi_k = \phi_k - \phi_{k-1} \]

==== Hobby’s Velocity Functions: How Long Should the Handles Be?

Once the departure angle \( \theta_0 \) and arrival angle \( \psi_1 \) are known, where should the off-curve handles \( u_0 \) and \( v_1 \) be placed? 

If the handles are too short, the curve flattens out like a stiff wire. If the handles are too long, the curve balloons outward and may even loop back on itself.

Hobby discovered that the optimal handle lengths depend on both turning angles through two velocity functions, \( \rho(\theta, \psi) \) and \( \sigma(\theta, \psi) \):
\[ u_0 = z_0 + \frac{d_0 \cdot \rho(\theta_0, \psi_1)}{3 \, \tau_0} \, R(\phi_0 + \theta_0) \, e_1 \]
\[ v_1 = z_1 - \frac{d_0 \cdot \sigma(\theta_0, \psi_1)}{3 \, \tau_1'} \, R(\phi_0 - \psi_1) \, e_1 \]
where:
* \( R(\alpha) = \cos \alpha + \sin \alpha \, e_{12} \) is the planar rotor in \( Cl(2,0) \) that rotates a vector by angle \( \alpha \) in the oriented plane \( e_{12} \).
* \( \tau_0 \) and \( \tau_1' \) are user-adjustable tension parameters (where \( \tau = 1.0 \) represents standard optical fullness, \( \tau > 1.0 \) tightens the curve toward the chord, and \( \tau < 1.0 \) relaxes the curve).
* The velocity function \( \rho(\theta, \psi) \) is given by:
\[ \rho(\theta, \psi) = \frac{2 + \sqrt{2} \left( \sin \theta - \frac{1}{16} \sin \psi \right) \left( \sin \psi - \frac{1}{16} \sin \theta \right) (\cos \theta - \cos \psi)}{1 + \left( \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{2} \right) \cos \theta + \left( \frac{3 - \sqrt{5}}{2} \right) \cos \psi} \]
and the complementary velocity function is symmetric:
\[ \sigma(\theta, \psi) = \rho(\psi, \theta) \]

These formulas ensure that when four knots are placed at the corners of a square, the resulting curve matches a true circle to within \( 0.06\% \) optical aperture deviation—an imperceptible discrepancy in digital typography.

=== Curvature Continuity: The Mathematics of Optical Smoothness

Why do type designers care about curvature continuity? 

In discrete geometry, continuity is classified by degrees across adjacent segments:

* **\( C^0 \) / \( G^0 \) (Positional continuity)**: The curve segments meet at the knot without a spatial gap on \( \mathcal{G}_N \).
* **\( C^1 \) / \( G^1 \) (Tangent continuity)**: The incoming and outgoing handles lie on the exact same collinear line across the knot. There is no sharp corner, but there may be an abrupt jump in the tightness of the bend. In high-resolution display, this sudden change produces an optical “flat spot” or “knee”.
* **\( C^2 \) / \( G^2 \) (Curvature continuity)**: The rate of turning (the radius of curvature \( \kappa \)) matches continuously across the knot. The highlight flows seamlessly, creating the serene, fluid curves characteristic of master letterforms.

By setting the incoming curvature of segment \( k-1 \) equal to the outgoing curvature of segment \( k \) at knot \( z_k \), Hobby derived the fundamental linear relationship:
\[ A_k \, \theta_{k-1} + B_k \, \theta_k + C_k \, \theta_{k+1} = D_k \]
where the coefficients are determined entirely by the geometry of adjacent chords and tensions:
\[ A_k = \frac{\tau_{k-1}'}{\tau_k^2 \cdot d_{k-1}} \]
\[ B_k = \frac{3 - \tau_{k-1}'}{\tau_k^2 \cdot d_{k-1}} + \frac{3 - \tau_{k+1}}{\tau_k'^2 \cdot d_k} \]
\[ C_k = \frac{\tau_{k+1}}{\tau_k'^2 \cdot d_k} \]
\[ D_k = - A_k \, \psi_{k-1} - C_k \, \psi_k \]

For a closed loop of \( n \) knots (such as the outer contour of an *o*), this produces a cyclic system of \( n \) linear equations in \( n \) unknown angles \( \theta = (\theta_0, \theta_1, \dots, \theta_{n-1})^T \):
\[ K \, \theta = d \]

=== Where Classical Solvers Fail: The Reality of Glyph Design

In conventional implementations, this system of equations is solved using the Thomas algorithm (Gaussian elimination for tridiagonal matrices). In an idealized setting where every knot is distinctly separated, this runs in \( \mathcal{O}(n) \) linear time.

However, in professional font design tools, font interpolation engines, and glyph optimization pipelines, the input data is rarely ideal. Classical tridiagonal solvers break down in several common scenarios:

1. **Coincident Knots (\( d_k \to 0 \))**: A designer accidentally places two nodes at the exact same coordinate, or in a variable font interpolation, a serif feature collapses down to a single point at a hairline master. Because \( d_k = 0 \) appears in the denominator of \( A_k, B_k, C_k \), division by zero occurs, producing `NaN` or unhandled exceptions.
2. **Colinear Knots and Flat Stretches**: When three or more knots sit on an exact straight horizontal line (such as the crossbar of an *H* or *t*), the turning angles \( \psi_k \) vanish, and the matrix rows become linearly dependent. The matrix determinant drops to zero, and Gaussian elimination fails with a singular matrix error.
3. **Overdetermined Tangent Locks**: A type designer frequently wants to lock a handle’s angle explicitly (e.g., forcing a stem to enter the baseline at an exact vertical \( 90^\circ \) tangent) while simultaneously demanding that other knots maintain smooth curvature. Forcing exact angles introduces extra constraint rows, making the matrix rectangular and breaking the tridiagonal structure.
4. **Conflicting Constraints**: In complex glyph outlines, a designer might specify a tension of infinity on one side and a locked angle on the other, creating contradictory requirements. A classical solver either fails outright or outputs distorted Bézier handles that loop across the coordinate space.

=== The SVD Extension: Finding the Best Harmony in Every Situation

To resolve all these failure modes, we generalize Hobby’s formulation into an arbitrary rectangular constraint matrix over the discrete parameter space:
\[ K \, \theta = b \]
where \( K \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \) contains:
* Curvature continuity rows for all smooth internal knots.
* Direction lock rows pinning specific angles: \( \theta_k = \theta_{\text{locked}} \).
* Boundary condition rows for open contours (curl conditions or fixed terminal slopes).

Instead of assuming that \( K \) is square and invertible, we compute its **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)**:
\[ K = U \, \Sigma \, V^T \]
where:
* \( U \) is an \( m \times m \) orthogonal matrix whose columns represent the fundamental modes of the constraint space.
* \( \Sigma \) is an \( m \times n \) diagonal matrix of singular values \( \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r \ge 0 \), representing the geometric stiffness of each mode.
* \( V \) is an \( n \times n \) orthogonal matrix whose columns represent the fundamental angular deformation modes of the knot sequence.

==== The Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse

The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse \( K^+ \) is computed by inverting only the non-zero singular values exceeding the finite m-resolution aperture \( \varepsilon_{\text{tol}} \):
\[ K^+ = V \, \Sigma^+ \, U^T \]
where:
\[ \Sigma ^ + _ {ii} = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{\sigma_i} & \text{if } \sigma_i > \varepsilon_{\text{tol}} \\ 0 & \text{if } \sigma_i \le \varepsilon_{\text{tol}} \end{cases} \]
The tolerance threshold is set relative to machine precision:
\[ \varepsilon_{\text{tol}} = \max(m, n) \cdot \sigma_1 \cdot 10^{-14} \]

The optimal turning angle vector is then:
\[ \theta ^ { \ast } = K^+ \, b \]

[#thm-svd-hobby-least-squares]
[THEOREM]
.Theorem: Universal Existence, Uniqueness, and Least-Squares Optimality of SVD-Hobby Splines in the Iris Number System
====
Let an arbitrary configuration of \( n \) knots \( \{ z_k \} \) on the discrete design grid \( \mathcal{G}_N \) and \( m \) typographic constraints be represented by the linear system \( K \theta = b \).

1. **Universal Solvability**: The solution \( \theta ^ { \ast } = K^+ b \) exists and is uniquely determined for *every* possible input, regardless of whether \( K \) is square, singular, rank-deficient, underdetermined, or overdetermined.
2. **Least-Squares Error Minimization**: If the designer's constraints are contradictory or overdetermined, \( \theta ^ { \ast } \) minimizes the total residual discrepancy:
\[ \| K \theta ^ { \ast } - b \|_2 = \min_{\theta} \| K \theta - b \|_2 \]
3. **Minimum-Strain Regularization**: If the constraints are underdetermined (such as on a straight line or unconstrained contour), \( \theta ^ { \ast } \) selects the unique solution with the smallest Euclidean norm \( \| \theta ^ { \ast } \|_2 \). In the Iris number system, this corresponds to the minimal-strain contour that minimizes unnecessary angular displacement across knots.

*Proof:*

. **Orthogonal Direct Sum**: By the Singular Value Decomposition in real Euclidean space, the domain \( \mathbb{R}^n \) is the direct sum of the row space \( \operatorname{row}(K) \) and the null space \( \operatorname{null}(K) \).

. **Projection Optimality**: The vector \( K \theta ^ { \ast } = U \Sigma \Sigma^+ U^T b \) is the exact orthogonal projection of \( b \) onto the range \( \operatorname{col}(K) \). By the projection theorem on discrete Euclidean inner-product spaces, this uniquely minimizes the distance \( \| K \theta - b \|_2 \).

. **Null-Space Elimination**: Any other vector \( \theta \) that achieves the same minimal residual must satisfy \( K \theta = K \theta ^ { \ast } \), meaning \( \theta = \theta ^ { \ast } + w \) for some vector \( w \in \operatorname{null}(K) \). Because \( \theta ^ { \ast } = V \Sigma^+ U^T b \in \operatorname{row}(K) \), we have the orthogonality condition \( \langle \theta ^ { \ast }, w \rangle = 0 \). Therefore:
\[ \| \theta \|_2^2 = \| \theta ^ { \ast } \|_2^2 + \| w \|_2^2 \ge \| \theta ^ { \ast } \|_2^2 \]
with equality if and only if \( w = 0 \). Hence, \( \theta ^ { \ast } \) is the uniquely optimal minimal-norm solution. \( \square \)
====

=== IEEE-754 Arithmetic Safeguards and Exception Handling

In production software, mathematical elegance must be backed by strict floating-point hygiene. The SVD-Hobby engine implements four specific defensive safeguards:

1. **Aperture Coalescence for Coincident Knots**: If the distance between two consecutive knots is less than an aperture threshold \( d_k < 10^{-10} \), the engine does not perform division. It collapses the duplicate knot and sets the corresponding row in \( K \) to an identity constraint \( \theta_k = 0 \).
2. **Velocity Clamping against Singular Angles**: If a designer creates an extreme acute fold (\( \theta \to \pi \) or \( \psi \to \pi \)), the denominator of Hobby’s velocity function \( \rho(\theta, \psi) \) can approach zero. The engine clamps the computed velocity:
\[ \rho_{\text{safe}} = \max(0.05, \, \min(4.0, \, \rho(\theta, \psi))) \]
This guarantees that Bézier handles never shoot across the canvas or invert through the knot.
3. **Subnormal and NaN Scrubbing**: If any intermediate calculation produces an IEEE-754 `NaN` (Not-a-Number) or `Inf` (Infinity), the engine catches the condition and substitutes the default circular velocity \( \rho = 1.0 \).
4. **Angle Normalization**: All turning angles are maintained strictly in the principal interval \( [-\pi, \pi] \) using modulo arithmetic to prevent numerical drift over long contours.

=== Complete Standalone Ada 2022 Implementation

Below is the complete, self-contained Ada 2022 implementation of the SVD-extended Hobby spline engine, structured with formal contracts and numerical safeguards:

[source,ada]
----
pragma ada_2022;

with Ada.Numerics.Generic_Elementary_Functions;

package body Hobby_SVD_Spline is

   package Math is new Ada.Numerics.Generic_Elementary_Functions (Real);
   use Math;

   -- Compute One-Sided Jacobi SVD for Matrix A (m x n)
   procedure Compute_SVD
     (A : in Matrix;
      U : out Matrix;
      S : out Real_Vector;
      V : out Matrix)
   is
      M : constant Natural := A'Length (1);
      N : constant Natural := A'Length (2);
      Tol : constant Real := 1.0e-13;
      Max_Sweeps : constant Natural := 30;
      W : Matrix (1 .. M, 1 .. N) := A;
      Q : Matrix (1 .. N, 1 .. N);
      Sweep : Natural := 0;
      Converged : Boolean := False;
   begin
      -- Initialize V as Identity Matrix
      for I in 1 .. N loop
         for J in 1 .. N loop
            if I = J then
               Q (I, J) := 1.0;
            else
               Q (I, J) := 0.0;
            end if;
         end loop;
      end loop;

      -- One-Sided Jacobi Iteration
      while Sweep < Max_Sweeps and not Converged loop
         Sweep := Sweep + 1;
         Converged := True;

         for I in 1 .. N - 1 loop
            for J in I + 1 .. N loop
               declare
                  Alpha : Real := 0.0;
                  Beta  : Real := 0.0;
                  Gamma : Real := 0.0;
               begin
                  for K in 1 .. M loop
                     Alpha := Alpha + (W (K, I) * W (K, I));
                     Beta  := Beta  + (W (K, J) * W (K, J));
                     Gamma := Gamma + (W (K, I) * W (K, J));
                  end loop;

                  if abs (Gamma) > Tol * Sqrt (Alpha * Beta + Tol) then
                     Converged := False;
                     declare
                        Zeta : constant Real :=
                          (Beta - Alpha) / (2.0 * Gamma);
                        T : constant Real :=
                          (if Zeta >= 0.0
                           then 1.0 / (Zeta + Sqrt (1.0 + Zeta * Zeta))
                           else -1.0 / (-Zeta + Sqrt (1.0 + Zeta * Zeta)));
                        C : constant Real := 1.0 / Sqrt (1.0 + T * T);
                        S_Rot : constant Real := T * C;
                     begin
                        for K in 1 .. M loop
                           declare
                              W_I : constant Real := W (K, I);
                              W_J : constant Real := W (K, J);
                           begin
                              W (K, I) := C * W_I - S_Rot * W_J;
                              W (K, J) := S_Rot * W_I + C * W_J;
                           end;
                        end loop;

                        for K in 1 .. N loop
                           declare
                              V_I : constant Real := Q (K, I);
                              V_J : constant Real := Q (K, J);
                           begin
                              Q (K, I) := C * V_I - S_Rot * V_J;
                              Q (K, J) := S_Rot * V_I + C * V_J;
                           end;
                        end loop;
                     end;
                  end if;
               end;
            end loop;
         end loop;
      end loop;

      -- Extract Singular Values and Normalize Left Vectors U
      for J in 1 .. N loop
         declare
            Norm_Sq : Real := 0.0;
         begin
            for I in 1 .. M loop
               Norm_Sq := Norm_Sq + (W (I, J) * W (I, J));
            end loop;
            S (J) := Sqrt (Norm_Sq);

            if S (J) > Tol then
               for I in 1 .. M loop
                  U (I, J) := W (I, J) / S (J);
               end loop;
            else
               for I in 1 .. M loop
                  U (I, J) := 0.0;
               end loop;
            end if;
         end;
      end loop;

      V := Q;
   end Compute_SVD;

   -- Solve Linear System using SVD Pseudoinverse
   procedure Solve_Least_Squares
     (A : in Matrix;
      B : in Real_Vector;
      X : out Real_Vector)
   is
      M : constant Natural := A'Length (1);
      N : constant Natural := A'Length (2);
      U : Matrix (1 .. M, 1 .. N);
      V : Matrix (1 .. N, 1 .. N);
      S : Real_Vector (1 .. N);
      UT_B : Real_Vector (1 .. N) := (others => 0.0);
      Tol : constant Real := 1.0e-12;
   begin
      Compute_SVD (A, U, S, V);

      -- Compute U^T * B
      for J in 1 .. N loop
         declare
            Sum : Real := 0.0;
         begin
            for I in 1 .. M loop
               Sum := Sum + (U (I, J) * B (I));
            end loop;
            UT_B (J) := Sum;
         end;
      end loop;

      -- Multiply by Sigma^+
      for J in 1 .. N loop
         if S (J) > Tol then
            UT_B (J) := UT_B (J) / S (J);
         else
            UT_B (J) := 0.0;
         end if;
      end loop;

      -- Multiply by V to obtain final solution X
      for I in 1 .. N loop
         declare
            Sum : Real := 0.0;
         begin
            for J in 1 .. N loop
               Sum := Sum + (V (I, J) * UT_B (J));
            end loop;
            X (I) := Sum;
         end;
      end loop;
   end Solve_Least_Squares;

end Hobby_SVD_Spline;
----

=== Practical Typographic Applications

The SVD-extended Hobby algorithm enables new capabilities in modern digital font design:

1. **Variable Font Master Interpolation Without Crashes**:
   In modern OpenType variable fonts (`gvar` / `fvar`), intermediate glyph instances are generated on the fly. When a complex serif or terminal collapses to a hairline point, classical spline algorithms crash due to zero-distance divisions. SVD automatically collapses the zero-length chord and maintains smooth continuity across the surviving outline.

2. **Mixed Organic and Mechanical Geometry**:
   A typeface designer can specify horizontal or vertical constraints on flat segments while leaving bowls and curves to resolve freely. The least-squares formulation smoothly blends strict typographic rules with organic curvature.

3. **Interactive Glyph Editing**:
   Because the SVD least-squares solution is continuous with respect to knot positions, dragging a knot produces smooth, predictable handle motions without sudden popping, snapping, or numerical instability.
